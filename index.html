Brick by Brick: 75 Years of LEGO's Data-Driven Transformation

FILE 01 / 03
Power BI · Interactive Time Capsule, 1949–2027

The Question

Most LEGO retrospectives are nostalgia pieces. I wanted to know whether 75 years of catalog data actually told a business story — specifically, whether one unchanged core system, the interlocking brick, could explain how the company grew without losing structural coherence.

The Build

I modeled the full public Rebrickable LEGO catalog — 27,069 records across 149 themes, 1949 to 2024 — as a proper star schema: a sets fact table joined to theme, color, and a fully custom date dimension, with bridge tables connecting parts and minifigures. Eight DAX measures on top, including a safely-divided average parts-per-set, power the ribbon, trend, and lifecycle views, plus an interactive year-by-year explorer.

What Surprised Me

LEGO grew from 5 sets in 1949 to 1,339 in a single year by 2025 — expected. What wasn't expected: 7,444 of those 27,069 records, nearly 1 in 3, aren't brick sets at all — they're keychains, bags, and stationery. The real transformation isn't the volume growth. It's that LEGO quietly became a lifestyle-merchandise brand.

Airlines Social Listening: From 2015 Complaints to Real-Time Sentiment

FILE 02 / 03
Power BI · Six Carriers, Jan–Jun 2026

The Question

What actually drives airline sentiment on social media — and is a single company-wide sentiment score even a useful number to report? I combined two datasets on purpose: a historical complaint set and a broader live-monitoring model, to answer both "what do people complain about" and "how is sentiment trending, and why."

The Build

I modeled six carriers (American, Delta, JetBlue, Southwest, Spirit, United) across two fact tables — tweet volume/engagement and topic-level sentiment — sharing common airline and date dimensions. 19 DAX measures cover volume, engagement rate, and sentiment-rate calculations, layered against the original 2015 Kaggle complaint dataset for ground-truth context.

What Surprised Me

The overall net sentiment score was a flat, unremarkable 3.9% — until I broke it out by airline and found a 29-point swing hiding underneath: Delta near +13%, Spirit near -16%. Reporting sentiment as one company-wide average erases the story entirely.

The 100 Year Story: How Theme Parks Built Central Florida's Economy

FILE 03 / 03
Power BI · 29 Parks, 1924–2024

The Question

In 1924, Central Florida drew about 20,000 visitors a year on orange-blossom rail tours. By 2024, the region drew 79.0 million. I wanted to model what actually built that economy — not just tourism headlines, but the jobs, ticket pricing, and infrastructure behind it.

The Build

I hand-built a dataset covering all 29 theme parks ever opened in the region (19 still open, 10 closed) — attendance, employment, ticket pricing, airport traffic, hotel rooms, and even workforce education programs — across a century. A shared year dimension connects visitor, employment, and population history through roughly 42 DAX measures, driving five dashboard views from park-level detail up to the regional growth story.

What Surprised Me

Since 1971 — when the first major park opened on 27,000 acres of swampland — theme park jobs grew 8.9x while visitors grew only 5.6x. Employment consistently outpaced visitation. This isn't really a service industry; it behaves like a manufacturing line whose only product is experience.
